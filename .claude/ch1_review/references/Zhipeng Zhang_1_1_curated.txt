1.1 Background and Motivation

     Cells are the smallest structural and functional units of all living organisms.

Discovered more than 300 years ago, cells have been a very challenging yet interesting

subject for researchers and till now we are still in the process of learning how cells work.

Cells are extremely complex systems that can perform a variety of functions such as cell

division, metabolism, and protein synthesis. As the functioning of cells is essentially

controlled by individual biomolecules and their interactions, it has been required to study

cells at nanometer scale whereas the studies have been limited by the availability of high

resolution technologies. The spatial resolution of traditional microscopy technologies is

limited by optical diffraction. With the advances in green fluorescent protein (GFP)

technologies and detection technologies such as fluorescence resonance energy transfer

(FRET) [1], it is now possible to observe the dynamics of biomolecules inside living cells.

These techniques have provided important insights into molecular processes. However,

they have the limitation that they only passively record the processes in cells.

       Single molecule manipulation techniques, such as AFM cantilevers, optical

tweezers, and magnetic tweezers, provide a new direction for biological research. These

manipulators not only make observations in real time, but also apply forces to actively

manipulate individual molecules. Although the single molecule manipulations are usually

performed in vitro, these manipulators can be applied to investigate cells in vivo at

nanometer scale, and have the potential to provide fundamentally new information for a

better understanding of cell function in their physiological condition. The acquired

knowledge will eventually contribute to not only the understanding of the physiology and

pathology, but also the development of pharmacy and discovery of new medicine. As a

result, the instrument capable of actuating, measuring, and controlling biological samples

at nanometer scale will significantly facilitate our study in biological science.



1.1.1 Actuation

       The single molecule manipulators developed so far can be classified into two

categories [2]: mechanical force transducers and external field manipulators. Examples in

the first category include atomic force microscopy (AFM) cantilevers [3,4], microneedles

[5-7], etc. They utilize a bendable cantilever to generate forces to samples, and these

manipulators have been successfully used in many applications. However, this method

suffers from the invasiveness of the mechanical cantilever, especially when probing

inside cells, as well as the inherent limitation in the degrees of freedom that the

mechanical force can be applied.

       Manipulators in the second category utilize field gradient to generate forces on

microscopic probes. These apparatuses are usually called “tweezers”. If molecules are

attached to the microscopic probes, biologically relevant forces can be applied to the

molecules through the probes. The probes can be introduced into a living cell without cell



                                              2
damage, hence are more suitable for in vivo manipulations. Furthermore, multi-axis

manipulation can be realized by varying the field gradient.

       Optical tweezers have received tremendous interest since it was first reported in

1986 [ 8 ]. It uses a focused laser beam to provide an optical force to microscopic

dielectric objects. Optical forces on the order of piconewton (pN) with near infrared (NIR)

laser beam were soon demonstrated for noninvasive trapping and manipulation of a single

living cell [9]. Optical tweezers have since proven to be a useful tool in many research

disciplines [10,11]. However, there are key barriers that inhibit its application to the study

of biological systems. The focus of the laser beam where the trapping happens has

tremendous high intensity power of light. A small portion of laser power absorbed by the

biological samples could significantly increase the local temperature [12,13], resulting in

photodamage to the samples. Moreover, as the optical force depends on the refractive

index mismatch between the dielectric object and the medium, the specificity of optical

tweezers is not satisfactory. Anything that resides inside the laser focus and that has a

different index from the medium will experience the optical force. The external force will

be a disturbance to the biological environment.

       Dielectrophoretic (DEP) traps [14], also called electric tweezers, use non-uniform

electric field to exert forces on dielectric particles. The direction of the force is

determined by the frequency-dependent dielectric constants of the medium and the

particle. Stable trapping has been realized in a certain frequency range with quadrupole

[ 15 ] or octopole [ 16 ] electrode arrangement. DEP forces have been successfully

employed in trapping biological samples [17,18], and sorting cells with different electric




                                              3
properties [19,20]. However, DEP traps, similar to optical tweezers, also suffer from the

lack-of-specificity problem.

       Magnetic tweezers, which employ magnetic field to actuate magnetic particles,

are more suitable for manipulating biological samples. Magnetic field is intangible and

safe to most biological materials, and has been successfully used in many biological

applications such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [21], magnetic drug targeting

[22,23] and magnetofection [24]. The magnetic force can be applied specifically to the

magnetic probes, and there is no heat generated in the process. Magnetic tweezers have

been studied since 1949 [ 25 ]. Since then, magnetic forces have been utilized to

investigate a wide range of biological samples. Most of these systems apply forces in a

single direction, using only one coil-actuated pole [26-29] or two poles facing each other

[30-32]. There are also examples of 2D manipulators implementing multiple poles [33-

37], and a few [38-41] are able to generate forces in 3D space. Overall, magnetic force, in

terms of specificity and biocompatibility, is the most suitable form of actuation for in

vivo biological study. Magnetic tweezers therefore are investigated in this research.



1.1.2 Measurement

       High-resolution motion detection of the microscopic probes is an important

component of the manipulators. As the molecules are usually attached to the probes, the

motions of the probes could be related to the dynamics and responses of the biological

samples. Hence, it is required for the measurement system to be able to track the

microscopic probes in real time with nanometer/subnanometer resolution. The laser

detection system, often found in optical tweezers’ setup [42,43], utilizes a quadrant



                                             4
photodiode (QPD) to record the interference pattern generated by the scattered light by

the particle and the unscattered light, and is able to provide three-dimensional (3D)

position information of the particle with subnanometer precision with MHz bandwidth.

The measurement range, however, is limited by the size of the laser focus, around 1 μm

in each axis. High-resolution motion devices thus are usually employed to keep the probe

inside the laser focus, at the expense of the instrument’s complexity. Fluorescence

microscopy [44-48] has also been used to locate and track particles in three dimensions.

By employing 4D microscopy [45-47], three dimensional tracking can be realized by

acquiring images at difference z positions and analyzing the image z-stacks. It is thus not

surprising that methods based on 4D microscopy suffer from poor temporal resolution. In

the case of total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM) [48], the particle

image intensity is associated with its axial location, as the illuminating field undergoes

exponential intensity decay with increasing distance from the coverslip surface.

Analyzing one image is therefore adequate to estimate the axial position of the target

particle. Both measurement speed and axial resolution of the methods based on TIRFM

are improved when compared with those of the 4D microscopy methods. However, the

axial measurement range is limited to be within 250nm above the surface of the coverslip

due to the working principle of TIRFM.

       Off-focus imaging methods [49-51], on the other hand, have the potential to

achieve a much larger axial measurement range while maintaining subnanometer

resolution by analyzing only one off-focus image. The measurement range is limited only

by the microscope’s field of view in x and y axes, and the axial range is several to tens of

micrometers depending on the numerical aperture (NA) of the objective lens. Moreover,



                                             5
the position of multiple beads can be measured simultaneously as long as they are in the

same field of view. Speidel et al. [49] used the radius of the outer bright ring of a

fluorescent particle in off-focus condition as a measure of z position. They found a linear

relationship between the radius and z position at the above-focus range, and

subnanometer resolution was achieved at less than 10Hz sampling rate. When a weak

cylindrical lens is introduced in the optical path [50,51], off-focus images become

elliptical. Then the z position of the particle could be determined from image shape and

orientation. Instead of using only certain specific features, this research presents a

systematic way to extract and use all the information of off-focus images to detect the z

position. In addition, other than approximate theoretical models, object-specific

calibration data and matching algorithm are used to locate the z location. The

measurement precision and accuracy therefore are both greatly improved. This algorithm

is first developed in bright field illumination and then extended to measure fluorescent

beads. The proposed method only requires the particle to be spherical, which is true for

many micrometer- and submicrometer-scale beads used in biological applications.



1.1.3 Feedback control

       One limitation of magnetic tweezers when compared with optical and electric

tweezers is that magnetic tweezers do not have a stable trap of the magnetic particle.

Therefore most of the applications of magnetic tweezers are performed in an open-loop

configuration. In other words, magnetic forces are applied according to the pre-calibrated

force model, and the induced motion of the magnetic particle is then recorded through

various sensing techniques. Feedback control, however, can be employed to extend the



                                            6
operation   versatility    and   the   manipulation   accuracy   of   magnetic   tweezers.

Kollmannsberger and Fabrry [52] established a force feedback to achieve precise force

application using their single-pole magnetic apparatus. With a six-pole instrument, Gosse

and Croquette [38] applied position feedback and realized a stable trapping of a 4.5µm

magnetic particle.

       Feedback control therefore is essential to magnetic tweezers. With the successful

implementation of feedback control, magnetic tweezers can be controlled to regulate the

motion and/or the force of the magnetic particle. According to application requirements,

advanced control algorithms can be developed, and operation versatility of the instrument

can be greatly enhanced.
