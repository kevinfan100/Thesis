1.1    Background and Motivation

       In the field of cell mechanics and mechanobiology, the application of scanning

probe microscopy (SPM) to scan biological samples and to manipulate biological

processes has become an important technique [1]. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) is

known for its high spatial resolution and force sensing capabilities and can be used to

scan the topography of biological samples, such as living cells. A typical AFM is a two-

dimensional surface tool that is mainly used to obtain micromechanical properties of

planar and surface topography [2]. For live-cell applications, a bead approximately one

micrometer in diameter is attached at the tip of the cantilever to reduce the stress exerted

on live cells, whereas the interaction force between the bead and the cell surface is at the

nanonewton scale [73]. Due to the limited indentation speed, the number of indentation

sites is usually small, thus the resolution of the acquired spatial distribution of the

micromechanical properties of the cell surface is limited [73].

       Optical trapping is another useful modern technique for the studies of biological

systems under physiological conditions [3-5]. Since the drag coefficient of a microscopic

particle (~     ) is significantly smaller than that of a micro-cantilever (>           ), an

optically trapped probing system can have sub-piconewton force resolution. However,

there are key barriers that hinder its application in the study of living biological systems.

Since the system requires greater force to trap small objects, it has been verified that the

heating problem caused by increasing laser power needs to be solved [6-8]. One feasible

approach is to implement real-time Brownian motion control to reduce heating [9].

Nevertheless, there is a doubt in the specificity of optical trapping in controlling the

probe. The probe can be easily mixed with unwanted trapping of debris. Despite these

problems, optical tweezers have gained popularity for their obvious advantage, i.e., a

stable trap is readily established for a microscopic dielectric object [10,11].

       Multipole electromagnetic actuators are widely used in a variety of applications,

including magnetic levitation of multi-axis motion devices [12,13], propulsion of

miniature swimming machines [14,15], and multi-axis scanning probe technology [16,17].

They have recently been implemented as magnetic tweezers that utilize electromagnets to

generate magnetic field to manipulate microprobes [18] and exert force on biological

samples ranging from sub-piconewtons to tens of piconewtons [19]. Magnetic

microprobes in magnetic fields are inherently unstable [36], so feedback control is

essential to achieve stable magnetic trapping. Thus, in the absence of active control,

magnetic particles are often anchored to molecules of interest to avoid instability. Most

existing magnetic tweezers are viewed as simple force applicators/transducers that apply

an external magnetic field to exert a force (       ) for quasi-static measurement of tensile

strength of or binding forces between molecules [20-22, 37-39]. Although these setups

are popular biophysical tools, i.e., manipulating individual macromolecules [40-43],

probing cell membranes [44-46], and measuring intracellular properties [33, 47-50], their

application is limited to simple experiments on isolated biological samples. Among

various magnetic tweezers, some were 2-D systems with multiple poles [51, 52]. The

development of a 2-D quadrupole magnetic tweezers was reported in [53, 54], wherein

four tip-shaped electromagnetic poles were employed to control 2-D magnetic force to

steer a microscopic magnetic particle.

       A hexapole electromagnetic actuator along with a visual servo control system was

developed to achieve 3-D magnetic actuation and stable trapping of a single microscopic

magnetic bead in aqueous solutions [43]. Recently, a newer hexapole actuator has been

developed, in which six pointed poles, each made of iron-steel rod and driven by separate

coils, and coils and poles are magnetically connected by a yoke to enhance the generation

of magnetic flux [23,24,74]. Hexapole magnetic actuating systems are 6-input/3-output

nonlinear actuators which are over-actuated systems.

       Optimal current allocation [25, 32] associated with hexapole actuation has been

devised to achieve the inverse modeling required for magnetic force generation. It solved

four problems in magnetic force generation, namely a) redundancy, b) coupling, c)

nonlinearity, and d) position-dependency. It was implemented in a Field Programmable

Gate Array (FPGA) system to realize real-time current allocation, thereby enabling

current-based control of the probing system. This illustrates the potential of using

magnetic tweezer technology to provide scanning probe functionality. Although current-

based control facilitates magnetic trapping and visual-servo control [23-25], magnetic

remanence and hysteresis could lead to uncertainties in magnetic flux generation. These

uncertainties increase as larger forced need to be generated. This leads to a greater force

error and reduces the performance of the actuation and the visual servo control system.
