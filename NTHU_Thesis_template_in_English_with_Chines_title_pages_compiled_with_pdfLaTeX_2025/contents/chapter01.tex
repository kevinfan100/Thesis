\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}

Accurate force generation and measurement at pico- to nano-Newton levels are essential for probing living cells and observing biological processes in aqueous solution. For this class of application, precision alone is not sufficient. The system must also preserve dynamic stability at high speed, suppress cross-axis interaction, and provide repeatable control behavior under practical operating conditions.

Among modern probing platforms, magnetic actuation is attractive because it enables remote and non-contact force generation with minimal optical burden. A Hall-sensor-based hexapole electromagnetic actuator further extends this capability to three-dimensional force control in an over-actuated configuration. Prior studies have already demonstrated key milestones, including the transition from current-based allocation to flux-based allocation, high-bandwidth flux regulation, and pico-Newton-level force control.

The remaining need is not another feasibility demonstration. The need is to clarify why the achieved performance is physically and systematically reasonable. In other words, the central motivation of this dissertation is to make the design rationale explicit: what tradeoffs are made, what problem each design step addresses, and how each step is verified.

This motivation starts from open-loop calibration of magnetic flux generation in the hexapole actuator. The measured responses indicate channel coupling, uncertainties caused by magnetic remanence and hysteresis, and frequency-dependent behavior in both gain and phase. These uncertainties become more pronounced when larger forces are required, which directly affects force-generation accuracy. Therefore, the investigation is organized as a linked chain from system identification to flux control and force generation, rather than as separate tasks.

Guided by these observations, this dissertation builds a traceable control chain from physical phenomena to model construction, from model construction to controller design, and from controller design to force-generation quality. The intended outcome is not only high performance, but also a reproducible decision path that explains why the performance is achieved and how it can be extended with confidence for ultra-precise high-speed untethered manipulation in aqueous solution.

\section{Research Objective and Scope}

The objective of this dissertation is to establish a traceable control-oriented framework for Hall-sensor-based hexapole actuation, and to show how design decisions propagate from model construction to final force behavior. The scope focuses on control-system design, analysis, and validation for magnetic manipulation in aqueous solution.

\textbf{Aim 1: System identification for control use.}
This aim establishes an identification workflow that converts open-loop physical observations into model information suitable for controller design. The investigation emphasizes how coupling, hysteresis-related behavior, and dynamic characteristics are represented for decision making. Simulation and experimental evidence are presented together in the same chapter to evaluate both model consistency and control relevance.

\textbf{Aim 2: Flux-control design and tradeoff analysis.}
This aim studies inner-loop flux control from a design-tradeoff perspective, including baseline PI control and model-based control structures. The focus is on physical interpretation of each control element and on decoupling quality in multi-axis operation. Simulation and experimental results are reported together to show where prediction and measured behavior agree or diverge, and why.

\textbf{Aim 3: Force-generation quality analysis.}
This aim examines the force-generation chain with emphasis on decoupling behavior, scheduling effects, and force-quality indicators. The purpose is to explain how upstream design choices affect downstream force output in a physically interpretable way. Simulation and experimental results are presented together within this thread rather than separated into a standalone result-only section.

The primary contribution is the explicit explanation of design tradeoffs and reproducible decision logic across the three aims. Workflow integration and automation are treated as supporting contributions. Claims are made under tested operating conditions, and broad biological interpretation is outside the scope of this dissertation.

\section{Dissertation Overview}

This dissertation is organized as follows. Chapter 1 introduces the background, motivation, objective, scope, and the three technical aims. Chapter 2 presents the modeling foundation required for subsequent control development, including force representation, allocation logic, and sensing relationships.

Chapter 3 develops the control-oriented system-identification thread and explains how open-loop observations are converted into design-relevant model information. Simulation and experimental analyses are presented in parallel to support interpretation.

Chapter 4 presents the flux-control thread, including baseline and model-based designs, component-level reasoning, and multi-axis decoupling behavior. Simulation and experimental evidence are discussed together in the same technical context.

Chapter 5 presents the force-generation thread and analyzes how allocation and scheduling choices influence force quality and cross-axis behavior. Simulation and experimental results are presented together to preserve causal interpretation across the chain.

Chapter 6 summarizes implementation-level integration and cross-layer validation. Chapter 7 discusses system-level extension and application-oriented implications. Chapter 8 concludes the dissertation and outlines future directions.
