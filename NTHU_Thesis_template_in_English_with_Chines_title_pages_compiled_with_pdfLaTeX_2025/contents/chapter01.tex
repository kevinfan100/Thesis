\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}

Accurate force generation and measurement at pico- to nano-Newton levels are essential for probing living cells and monitoring active biological processes in aqueous solution. For this class of application, force precision alone is not sufficient; the system must also sustain high-speed operation, maintain stable closed-loop behavior, and remain repeatable across experiments. Scanning-probe technologies have enabled major advances in mechanobiology, but each platform has practical boundaries: AFM provides high-resolution local force/topography sensing with contact constraints \cite{binnig1986afm,neuman2008singlemolecule}, optical tweezers provide flexible non-contact manipulation with optical-path and force-range tradeoffs \cite{ashkin1986observation,neuman2008singlemolecule}, and magnetic tweezers provide a strong alternative for long-term operation in aqueous conditions with reduced photodamage concerns \cite{gosse2002magnetic,devlaminck2012magnetic}. These comparisons motivate a control-centered magnetic platform that can combine force authority, dynamic bandwidth, and reproducible operation in one architecture.

Within magnetic manipulation, the hexapole electromagnetic approach has established a strong technical baseline. Early work progressed from quadrupole force modeling and implementation \cite{zhang2010quadrupole} to three-dimensional hexapole actuation with explicit magnetic-force modeling and over-actuated allocation structure \cite{zhang2011design}. This line of work showed that a six-input architecture can support three-dimensional force objectives, but it also made clear that practical performance is tightly coupled to how the redundant channels are allocated and how the magnetic generation process is modeled for control use. In other words, the platform capability is not determined by geometry alone; it depends on the quality of the engineering path from physical modeling to control synthesis.

The next phase focused on platform maturation through visual tracking, system integration, and hardware evolution \cite{zhang2013visual,long2016actively,long2016dissertation}. These studies expanded practical operation and demonstrated that the platform can be integrated for closed-loop experiments in aqueous settings. At the same time, improved actuation capability exposed the limitations of purely current-based modeling assumptions, especially under remanence- and hysteresis-related effects, where identical current commands may no longer produce consistent magnetic-flux responses across operating histories \cite{long2022hallsensor}. This is a key engineering turning point: hardware improvement alone does not guarantee predictable control performance unless the measured control variable and the model interface are aligned.

Recent work addressed this bottleneck through two major advances: optimization-based allocation and Hall-sensor-based flux control. Optimal current allocation improved force-efficiency and usable force range in over-actuated operation \cite{long2021optimal}. More importantly, Hall-sensor integration enabled direct magnetic-flux measurement and shifted the control architecture from current-based estimation to flux-based feedback \cite{long2022hallsensor}. Building on this architecture, high-bandwidth flux-control design, force-level regulation, and tracking-related validation were demonstrated with progressively stronger agreement between design expectation and experimental behavior \cite{meng2023ultraprecise,meng2024piconewton,meng2025nearwall}. These results show feasibility and high performance; however, they also reveal that the development path remains technically dense and strongly dependent on accumulated platform-specific implementation decisions.

Therefore, the remaining need is not another isolated performance demonstration. The key need is to formalize the engineering logic that connects model construction, controller synthesis, force-output analysis, and motion-level validation into a reusable methodology. The motivation of this dissertation is to make design rationale explicit and verifiable: what physical issue each stage addresses, what tradeoff is introduced, what evidence supports each decision, and how outputs from one stage are handed to the next stage without ambiguity. With this viewpoint, automation is treated as the objective, and systematic stage definition is treated as the prerequisite. Guided by this motivation, this dissertation follows one linked technical route: system identification, magnetic flux control, force generation, and motion control, so that the final contribution is not only a set of results but also a reproducible path for developing Hall-sensor-based hexapole systems under tested operating conditions.

\section{Research Objective and Scope}

The objective of this dissertation is to establish a generalizable automation framework for Hall-sensor-based hexapole magnetic tweezers by defining, implementing, and validating a stage-wise engineering workflow that starts from open-loop measurement and model construction, proceeds through magnetic-flux controller design and force-generation analysis, and culminates in force/motion output verification on the integrated platform. Instead of summarizing this objective as a single abstract chain, this work explicitly specifies the design interfaces, decision criteria, and evidence contracts at each stage, so that each design result can be regenerated, rechecked, and reused in subsequent development.

In practical terms, the research target is to turn platform-specific know-how into a transferable development procedure for compatible hexapole architectures. This means the dissertation emphasizes methodological clarity as much as final performance: model artifacts must be controller-ready, control parameters must have interpretable physical roles, and simulation/experiment comparisons must be organized to explain why a design choice is accepted or rejected. The intended outcome is a framework that supports both technical rigor and implementation efficiency in future deployments.

\textbf{Aim 1: System identification for control use.}
This aim establishes an identification workflow that converts open-loop physical observations into model information directly usable for controller design. The key question is how coupling, hysteresis-related effects, and frequency-dependent dynamics should be represented so that identification outputs preserve control-relevant behavior rather than fitting score alone. The expected output is a compact and interpretable model interface that can be handed to flux-control design with minimal ambiguity. Simulation and experimental evidence are presented in the same technical thread to evaluate both model consistency and control usefulness.

\textbf{Aim 2: Flux-control design and tradeoff analysis.}
This aim studies inner-loop magnetic-flux control from a design-tradeoff perspective, including baseline PI control and model-based control structures with feedforward and disturbance-related compensation. The key question is which controller component addresses which physical issue, and how these choices influence bandwidth, phase behavior, and multi-axis decoupling quality. The objective is not only to improve response quality but also to make component-level design logic explicit and reusable. Simulation and experimental evidence are presented together for direct comparison with design intent.

\textbf{Aim 3: Force-generation quality analysis.}
This aim examines the force-generation stage with emphasis on decoupling behavior, scheduling effects, and force-quality indicators under representative operating conditions. The key question is how upstream modeling and flux-control choices constrain downstream force-output quality and cross-axis interactions. The analysis focuses on interpretable links between design assumptions and observed force behavior, rather than reporting force metrics as isolated outcomes. Simulation and experimental evidence are presented in one continuous thread to preserve interpretability.

\textbf{Aim 4: Motion-control validation on the integrated platform.}
This aim validates motion-control behavior after force-generation and flux-control interfaces are established and verified. The key question is how decisions made in identification, flux-control, and force-generation stages propagate to tracking-level behavior and operational stability in application-oriented tasks. This aim provides the final integration check that connects inner-loop control quality to task-level performance. Simulation and experimental evidence are presented under the same pipeline to maintain stage-to-stage consistency.

The scope of this dissertation focuses on control-oriented workflow construction, validation logic, and automation interfaces for Hall-sensor-based hexapole magnetic tweezers. Mechatronic integration is included as enabling evidence required for reproducible calibration and closed-loop validation.

The overall purpose is to establish a generalizable and reproducible automation framework grounded in tested operating conditions and compatible architecture assumptions.

\section{Dissertation Overview}

This dissertation is organized as follows. Chapter 1 defines the motivation, objective, scope, and claim boundary of the automation-oriented framework.

Chapter 2 presents the system foundation, including the Hall-sensor-based hexapole architecture, mechatronic real-time implementation, optical/visual integration, and software-FPGA operation interfaces that support subsequent technical chapters.

Chapter 3 develops the system-identification thread and demonstrates how open-loop observations are transformed into control-use model information. Simulation and experimental analyses are presented together in the same technical context.

Chapter 4 presents the flux-control thread, including baseline and model-based design choices, component-level interpretation, and multi-axis decoupling behavior. Simulation and experimental evidence are discussed together for direct comparison with design intent.

Chapter 5 presents the force-generation and motion-control threads and analyzes how allocation and scheduling choices influence force quality, cross-axis behavior, and tracking-level outcomes. This chapter also traces design decisions from identification to flux control and force/motion outputs, and provides concluding remarks and future directions.

Together, Chapters 3 through 5 provide one continuous verification path from observed actuator behavior to design decisions and final force/motion quality.
